Week 7: Bank Runs

Beliefs, fundamentals, and bank failure

Setup: you can wait, but will you?

You deposited $1. You can wait until the bank’s loans mature, or join the early withdrawal queue. Compare what happens when beliefs change and when loans lose value.

The bank

100 depositors put in $1 each. The bank invests the $100 in long-term loans.

Selling $1 of loans early raises $0.90. In the first exercise, holding it to maturity returns $1.50.

The deposit contract

30 depositors need cash now. The other 70, including you, can wait.

The bank promises $1 now or $1.40 at maturity. Payments depend on the assets available.

Why banks are vulnerable

Depositors can ask for cash before loans mature. Early withdrawals force the bank to sell loans and give up future income. This exercise uses a simplified contract to show that mechanism.

Diamond–Dybvig: same assets, different expectations

The loans pay $1.50 at maturity in both cases. Choose what to do when you expect the other patient depositors to wait, then when you expect them to run.

The idea

Waiting is attractive when others wait. Running is attractive when others run. Diamond–Dybvig shows how both outcomes can be equilibria with the same fundamentals.

A withdrawing depositor joins a random queue. If the bank runs out of assets, some receive nothing. The game reports the average payment across queue positions.

Goldstein–Pauzner: what does your signal tell you?

Now loan quality varies. You see a signal, x = θ + noise, about the bank’s true strength θ. A higher number means stronger loans. Your signal also gives you a clue about what other depositors may have seen.

Three fixed practice cases with simulated depositors. Signals have errors of up to 10 points. Other patient depositors withdraw below 70 and wait at 70 or above. We assume this rule for the exercise; the paper derives its equilibrium cutoff.

What changes?

Both models have a coordination problem. Goldstein–Pauzner adds noisy private information about fundamentals. Under their assumptions, this selects a unique equilibrium with a signal cutoff: withdraw below it and wait above it.

A low signal suggests weaker loans and more withdrawals by others. Runs can still force costly liquidation at a bank that could repay if patient depositors waited.

A run and a bank failure are different events

Click each case. Separate the quality of the loans from the pressure to pay depositors now.

What does Verner’s research find?

Correia, Luck, and Verner find that bank failures are strongly linked to weak fundamentals. Their historical evidence also shows runs on strong banks, which often survive through support from other banks, signals of strength, or temporary suspension.

Baron, Verner, and Xiong find severe banking distress even without panics. Asset losses and runs deserve separate attention.

This evidence helps judge which mechanisms matter in practice. It does not by itself establish the specific equilibrium-selection mechanism in Goldstein–Pauzner.

The distinction

Liquidity: can the bank pay now? Solvency: are its assets worth enough to cover what it owes? A loan can bridge a cash shortage. It also has to be repaid, so it does not replace lost capital.

Keep these ideas in mind

Diamond–Dybvig

The same bank can have a waiting equilibrium and a run equilibrium. Expectations about others’ withdrawals matter.

Goldstein–Pauzner

Private signals link beliefs to fundamentals. Under the model’s assumptions, a unique equilibrium determines the withdrawal cutoff and run risk.

Verner and coauthors

Weak fundamentals help explain failures. A run can amplify distress; observing a run alone does not reveal what caused the bank’s problems.

How the payments are calculated

The bank starts with $100 in loans and no cash. Let n be the number of early withdrawals. It sells n ÷ 0.90 of loans, up to its total of $100. Early claimants receive up to $1 each in random order.

The average early payment is min(1, 90 ÷ n). Remaining loans pay R at maturity. Each waiting depositor receives min(1.40, remaining loans × R ÷ number waiting). Surplus belongs to the bank’s owners.

In the first exercise, R = 1.50. In the signal exercise, R = 0.90 + 0.006 × θ. The 69 simulated patient peers have evenly spaced signal errors between −10 and +10. Another 30 depositors always need cash early. Your choice determines the final withdrawal count.

The signal cutoff of 70 is a teaching assumption, not an estimated or solved Goldstein–Pauzner cutoff. A signal cutoff and a bank’s cash limit measure different things. These finite examples illustrate incentives; the original papers derive equilibrium under fuller assumptions.

The support case uses an idealized loan at zero interest, secured by the full maturity value of the assets. The bank repays the lender before paying waiting depositors. Actual lenders may lend less against the same assets.
