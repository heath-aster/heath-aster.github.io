Week 11 student explanation

Read this on its own or alongside the Sudden-Stop Policy Council. It contains the setting, complete example, formulas, policy choices and reflection task.

Open the activity

Understand the problem

Foreign investors want their money back. How can a policy council meet the demand for dollars while limiting harm to borrowers and households?

Start with USD 20 billion in reserves, USD 40 billion in external debt and a 5% policy rate. All money amounts below are USD billions (USD B). Each scenario describes one illustrative crisis episode, with no specified calendar duration.

Reduce the outflow

A rate rise may keep funds in the country but raises borrowing costs. Controls restrict a share of outflows. This model stops 60% of the covered amount.

Provide dollars

Reserve sales use an existing buffer. IMF borrowing supplies new dollars and creates new debt. Any unused dollars remain in reserves.

First learn the arithmetic, then choose policies for a USD 10B outflow and a separate USD 20B stress scenario. Both start from the same balance sheet. There are no random draws or sequential rounds. The stress case also has stronger bank and consumption sensitivities.

Your objective is to explain a defensible tradeoff. There is no single score or universally best policy. Completion and a written reflection support participation. Hints, mistakes and economic outcomes do not reduce credit.

Example: follow the dollars

Keep one policy in mind: sell USD 4B of reserves. Raise rates by 200 bps. Cover 30% of outflows with controls. Borrow USD 2B from the IMF. All coefficients are classroom assumptions, not estimated effects.

  1. Convert the rate: 100 basis points = 1 percentage point. Thus h = 200 / 100 = 2 pp. The rate rises from 5% to 7%.
  2. Reduce the original USD 10B outflow: controls stop 10 × 0.30 × 0.60 = 1.8B. Rates stop 0.8 × 2 = 1.6B. Remaining outflow A = max(0, 10 − 1.8 − 1.6) = 6.6B.
  3. Meet it with dollars: 4 + 2 = 6B of financing. Gap G = max(0, 6.6 − 6) = 0.6B. Unused funds U = max(0, 6 − 6.6) = 0B.
  4. Update the stocks: reserves = 20 − 4 + 0 = 16B. Debt = 40 + 2 = 42B. Funds spent on outflows are counted once.
  5. Find the currency change: f = −1.5 × 0.6 = −0.90%. This is the change in the USD value of one domestic-currency unit. The depreciation magnitude d = |f| = 0.90.
  6. Trace the costs: NPL share = 4 + 0.9 × 2 = 5.80% of loans (the extra 3 pp applies only above 10% depreciation). Consumption change = −(1.5 + 0.6 × 2 + 0.2 × 0.90) = −2.88%.

Next choice: another USD 0.6B of financing would close this gap, but using reserves or borrowing changes a different balance sheet. With the available 0.5B steps, an extra 0.5B leaves a 0.1B gap. An extra 1B leaves 0.4B unused.

What changes in the USD 20B stress scenario?

Keep exactly the same policy and reset the initial balance sheet. A = 20 × (1 − 0.6 × 0.30) − 0.8 × 2 = 14.8B. G = 14.8 − 4 − 2 = 8.8B. Currency change = −1.6 × 8.8 = −14.08%. Reserves and debt remain 16B and 42B.

NPL share = 4.5 + 1.2 × 2 + 0.3 × 14.08 = 11.124%, shown as 11.12%. Consumption change = −(2 + 0.8 × 2 + 0.25 × 14.08) = −7.12%. The stress case replaces the baseline NPL threshold rule.

Model and units

R = reserve sales (USD B). M = IMF borrowing (USD B). b = rate rise (bps). h = b/100 (percentage points). c = control percentage/100 (fraction). O = original outflow, 10B or 20B. Starting stocks are reserves 20B and debt 40B.

Outflow after controls and rates, A (USD B)
max(0, O × (1 − 0.6c) − 0.8h). Controls reduce the covered flow with 60% effectiveness. Rates reduce outflows by 0.8B per pp.
Gap G and unused financing U (USD B)
G = max(0, A − R − M). U = max(0, R + M − A). At most one is positive.
Ending stocks (USD B)
Reserves = 20 − R + U. External debt = 40 + M.
Currency change f (%). Depreciation magnitude d = |f|
Baseline: f = −1.5G. Stress: f = −1.6G. The quote is USD per unit of domestic currency, so negative means depreciation. This linear rule is an approximation, floored at zero pressure. Surplus funds do not cause appreciation.
NPL share (% of loans)
Baseline: 4 + 0.9h + 3 × indicator(d > 10). Stress: 4.5 + 1.2h + 0.3d. These are shares with percentage-point increments. NPLs are not default probabilities or currency losses.
Consumption change (% relative to pre-shock level)
Baseline: −(1.5 + 0.6h + 0.2d). Stress: −(2 + 0.8h + 0.25d). These are signed percentage changes, not dollar losses.

Precision: calculations use full precision. Money is displayed to two decimals in USD B, and percentages to two decimals. Practice accepts answers rounded to two decimals (and exact answers). Percentage fields accept a trailing % sign. A comma must be a thousands separator, not a decimal separator.

Limits: the model is a static teaching exercise with no probabilities, calibrated causal effects, or welfare ranking. It omits the longer-term costs of controls, IMF conditions, reserve adequacy targets, nonlinear flight responses and debt-service schedules. A dollar debt stock is distinct from a domestic-currency debt burden or today's cash requirement. The baseline NPL threshold is deliberately discontinuous. Zero depreciation does not mean a costless policy.

Compare and reflect

Choose each policy for the 10B and 20B cases. Compare each policy under both cases against no intervention, the maximum of each tool used alone, and the example mix. Hold the scenario fixed when comparing decisions. No policy is declared universally best. Keep currency stability, reserves, debt, bank stress and consumption separate.

Explain one policy change for stress, cite an outcome in each scenario, identify who bears a cost, and name an omitted cost that could change your conclusion. Practise each calculation or review its solution, review both decisions and Compare, then submit your reflection with your name, ID and section.
