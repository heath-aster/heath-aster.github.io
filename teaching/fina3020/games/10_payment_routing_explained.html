Payment routes and their full cost

A payment decision combines cost, arrival time, cash commitment and access. This explanation gives the inputs, calculations and interpretation without requiring you to play the game.

1. Understand the problem

Your task: arrange a payment from a Hong Kong firm to an overseas supplier. The supplier must receive USD 1,000,000 in full within 2 business days. The firm has HKD cash and pays all charges on top of the invoice.

Choose among three routes. First learn the cost calculation. Then test how the decision changes with payment size, delay risk and access.

Payment principal is the amount owed to the supplier. Prefunding is cash set aside before settlement. PvP releases one currency payment only when the other currency payment is made. A basis point (bp) is 0.01%, or 0.0001 as a decimal.

Route A

Correspondent banks

Your bank sends a SWIFT message. The money moves through prefunded nostro and vostro accounts at one or more correspondent banks.

Route B

PvP settlement

A settlement system pays the USD leg only if the HKD leg is also paid (payment versus payment). Netting means the desk prefunds only 20% of the payment.

Fixed fee
USD 400 per payment
Conversion spread
4 bp: HKD to USD in a PvP trade
Cash commitment
20% of principal for 1 day
Settlement-risk rule
Zero FX principal-risk charge on the covered PvP trade
Arrival
Same day, before the cut-off
Route C

Stablecoin bridge

Your firm buys a USD stablecoin, sends the token on a blockchain, and an off-ramp provider sells it for USD paid into the supplier’s bank.

Fixed fee
USD 50 per payment
Conversion spread
3 bp: On-ramp: HKD to a USD stablecoin
3 bp: Off-ramp: stablecoin to USD in the supplier’s bank
Cash commitment
100% of principal for 0.05 days
Settlement-risk rule
1 bp of principal for redemption risk
Arrival
About 1 hour

Common rate card: USD 100 per day per USD 1M of committed cash. Route A’s settlement-risk charge is 2.5 bp of principal per day of gap. All numbers are simplified classroom assumptions.

The carry day is a model time unit. Route C’s 0.05 day is about one hour (1.2 hours on a 24-hour convention). Actual business-day cut-offs and holidays are outside the arithmetic.

All three routes deliver USD bank money in this example. Route C uses a token between its two conversions. The firm’s contract still specifies USD.

2. Work through USD 1M

Question: What does each route cost above the supplier’s USD 1M? Costs are expressed in USD equivalent so we can add them. The invoice principal is identical for every route and is excluded from the total below.

1. FX conversion cost

Multiply the payment by each conversion spread, then add the legs. One bp = 1/10,000.

A: 1,000,000 × 8/10,000 = USD 800
B: 1,000,000 × 4/10,000 = USD 400
C: 1,000,000 × (3 + 3)/10,000 = USD 600

Both of C’s legs use the same USD principal as a small-spread approximation. Costs are paid separately so the supplier receives the full amount.

2. Cost of committed cash

First find committed cash, then apply its time and carry rate.

Carry = (principal × committed share / 1,000,000) × days × USD 100
A: (1,000,000 × 1 / 1,000,000) × 2 × 100 = USD 200
B: (1,000,000 × 0.20 / 1,000,000) × 1 × 100 = USD 20
C: (1,000,000 × 1 / 1,000,000) × 0.05 × 100 = USD 5

The carry rate is equivalent to 0.01% per day (3.6% simple annual interest on a 360-day basis). It differs from the optional lecture example’s 5% annual rate.

3. Assumed risk charge

A: principal × daily risk rate × gap days
1,000,000 × 2.5/10,000 × 2 = USD 500
B: USD 0 FX principal-risk charge for the covered PvP trade
C: 1,000,000 × 1/10,000 = USD 100 redemption-risk charge

The charge is a pricing assumption, not a default probability or an expected loss estimate. PvP does not remove liquidity or operational risk. Prefunding time and settlement gap are different inputs even though A uses two days for both.

5. A five-day delay and a future uncertain payment

Keep fees and FX spreads fixed. A’s committed time and gap both rise from 2 to 5 days. B and C remain unchanged in this scenario.

Extra carry: (5 − 2) × USD 100 = USD 300
Extra risk charge: 1,000,000 × 0.00025 × (5 − 2) = USD 750
A delayed: 1,650 + 300 + 750 = USD 2,700
At a 20% delay probability: 0.80 × 1,650 + 0.20 × 2,700 = USD 1,860

The two outcomes cost 1,650 and 2,700; 1,860 is their expected value. If a separate production loss L occurs only when late, add p × L to the expected cost. Do not count the baseline costs twice.

3. Practise with help

Use these five examples once, then let the game repeat the arithmetic. Enter the units requested. Commas are accepted; round currency to cents or the nearest dollar for whole-dollar answers.

Convert basis points into a dollar cost

Route A converts USD 1,000,000 equivalent at an 8 bp spread. What is the FX cost in USD?

Relationship: A basis point is one hundredth of one percentage point.

FX cost = principal × basis points ÷ 10,000.

Solution and meaning: 8 bp = 0.08% = 0.0008. USD 1,000,000 × 0.0008 = USD 800. This spread is paid in addition to the fixed fee.

Check: Divide 8 by 10,000, then multiply by USD 1M. Enter dollars, not 0.08 or 8.

Charge only the cash actually committed

Route B commits 20% of USD 1M for 1 day. Carry costs USD 100 per day per USD 1M committed. What is the carry cost?

Relationship: First find 20% of the payment. The rate applies to that amount.

Carry = (principal × committed share ÷ USD 1M) × days × USD 100.

Solution and meaning: USD 1M × 20% = USD 200,000 committed. (200,000 ÷ 1,000,000) × 1 × USD 100 = USD 20. Netting reduces the cash commitment; the supplier still receives USD 1M.

Check: Apply the rate to USD 200,000, not to the full payment. The payment and the committed cash are different amounts.

Apply the assumed settlement-risk charge

Route A has a 2-day settlement gap. The desk charges 2.5 bp of USD 1M per day of gap. What is the risk charge?

Relationship: The quote is per day. Include both days of exposure.

Risk charge = principal × (2.5 ÷ 10,000) × gap days.

Solution and meaning: USD 1,000,000 × 0.00025 × 2 = USD 500. This is a classroom pricing charge, not a default probability or a realised principal loss.

Check: 2.5 bp = 0.025% = 0.00025. Multiply by principal and by two days.

Add the four cost lines

For Route A, the fixed fee is USD 150, FX cost 800, carry 200 and risk charge 500. What is the total cost above the USD 1M payment?

Relationship: Add the four charges. The supplier payment is a separate amount.

Total cost = fixed fee + FX cost + carry + risk charge.

Solution and meaning: 150 + 800 + 200 + 500 = USD 1,650. The invoice principal is excluded from this cost comparison because it is the same for every route.

Check: Include every cost line once. Do not add USD 1M to an answer asking for costs above principal.

Weight costs by the chance of each outcome

A future Route A payment costs USD 1,650 with 80% probability or USD 2,700 with 20% probability. There is no additional production loss. What is its expected cost?

Relationship: An expected cost is a probability-weighted average of the two possible costs.

Expected cost = (1 − p) × on-time cost + p × delayed cost.

Solution and meaning: 0.80 × 1,650 + 0.20 × 2,700 = 1,320 + 540 = USD 1,860. Equivalently, 1,650 + 0.20 × (2,700 − 1,650). One payment costs 1,650 or 2,700 in this model; 1,860 is the average before its outcome is known.

Check: Enter 20% as 0.20. Weight both outcomes, or add only the expected extra delay cost to the baseline.

4. Make the two route decisions

Choose a baseline route, then choose for a new payment after A’s delay is known. A costs USD 2,700 and arrives in five days, missing the two-business-day deadline. B costs USD 820 and C USD 755, with unchanged access and quotes assumed. You cannot undo an already-sent payment by changing your answer.

C has the smallest baseline modeled cost. B costs USD 65 more and uses PvP on the covered FX trade. A has a lower fixed fee than B but a higher full cost. Access, cut-offs, redemption and liquidity risk can change which route is feasible.

Optional treasury workshop

These preserve the lecture’s additional calculation types. Bank A / Provider B / Provider C here are separate hypothetical quotes from the route game. Fixed-recipient quotes require the supplier to receive USD 100,000: A debits HKD 785,080, B HKD 782,030 and C HKD 780,830. A and B arrive in one day; C in three, beyond the two-day deadline.

Fixed recipient amount

Deliver exactly USD 100,000. Provider B quotes 7.80 HKD/USD, a 0.25% markup on that rate, and an HKD 80 sender fee. What is the total HKD debit?

HKD/USD tells you to multiply USD by the exchange rate.

HKD debit = USD received × HKD/USD × (1 + markup) + HKD fees.

100,000 × 7.80 = HKD 780,000. Markup = 780,000 × 0.0025 = HKD 1,950. Debit = 780,000 + 1,950 + 80 = HKD 782,030. The supplier receives the full USD 100,000.

Check: Use 0.0025 for 0.25%. Multiply by HKD/USD. Fees are paid separately by the sender.

Expected production loss

Provider B has a 5% chance of being late. A late payment causes HKD 100,000 of production loss; otherwise the loss is zero. What is the expected loss?

Multiply the loss if late by its probability.

Expected production loss = probability late × loss if late.

0.05 × HKD 100,000 = HKD 5,000. Add this to the certain debit: 782,030 + 5,000 = HKD 787,030. A realised loss is either zero or 100,000.

Check: 5% is 0.05. This question asks for the loss expectation, not the total sender debit.

Break-even loss per late payment

Bank A costs HKD 785,080 and is late with probability 0.5%. Provider B costs HKD 782,030 and is late with probability 5%. At what loss per late payment do their expected totals tie?

A costs more up front but has a lower probability of the same loss.

Loss at a tie = (A debit − B debit) ÷ (B delay probability − A delay probability).

(785,080 − 782,030) ÷ (0.05 − 0.005) = 3,050 ÷ 0.045 = HKD 67,777.78. The probability gap is 4.5 percentage points. B is cheaper below this loss; A is cheaper above it.

Check: Subtract the two probabilities in decimal form. A 4.5 percentage-point gap is 0.045, not 4.5.

Prefunding at an annual interest rate

Commit USD 500,000 for 3 days. The annual opportunity cost is 5%. Use simple interest and a 360-day year. What is the cost?

An annual rate must be scaled to three days.

Cost = committed cash × annual rate × days ÷ days per year.

500,000 × 0.05 × 3 ÷ 360 = USD 208.33. This separate lecture example uses a 5% annual rate. The route game uses its stated USD 100 per USD 1M per day rate card.

Check: Use the committed USD 500,000 and 3/360 of a year. Do not apply 5% as a daily rate.

Fixed sender budget

Your total budget is HKD 78,000. The broker route deducts HKD 10 first, converts the remainder at 7.80 HKD/USD, then deducts a USD 2 commission. How many USD remain?

Subtract each fee while the balance is in that fee’s currency.

Net USD = (HKD budget − HKD fee) ÷ (HKD/USD) − USD fee.

(78,000 − 10) ÷ 7.80 = USD 9,998.7179. Subtract USD 2: USD 9,996.72. The bank alternative gives (78,000 − 150) ÷ 7.84 = USD 9,929.85. The broker leaves USD 66.87 more, if both arrive in time.

Check: Divide HKD by HKD/USD to obtain USD. Deduct the USD commission after conversion.

Net reciprocal invoices

Europe owes Hong Kong EUR 500,000. Hong Kong owes Europe EUR 320,000 on the same date. Both claims may be netted. How much must Europe transfer?

Offset the smaller obligation against the larger one.

Net payment = 500,000 − 320,000.

Europe sends EUR 180,000. At a proportional 0.4% fee, gross fees are (500,000 + 320,000) × 0.004 = EUR 3,280; net fees are 180,000 × 0.004 = EUR 720; saving = EUR 2,560. Both invoices are discharged by agreement.

Check: Use the difference for the net transfer. Add the gross amounts only when calculating the original gross fees.

Cash needed before settlement

A fund has USD 1M now, posts USD 0.8M collateral today, and must pay USD 2M tomorrow. A USD 3M receipt arrives on day 2. No credit is available. What extra cash is needed by tomorrow?

Only cash available before tomorrow’s payment can fund it.

Shortfall = tomorrow’s payment − (initial cash − today’s collateral).

1 − 0.8 = USD 0.2M available. Tomorrow needs 2 − 0.2 = USD 1.8M extra. The later USD 3M receipt cannot fund an earlier payment without credit.

Check: Enter millions, such as 1.8. Do not use the day-2 receipt to pay tomorrow’s bill.

Payment rails and currency use

Liu’s transaction framework separates pricing, financing, risk transfer, payment and reinvestment. A supplier can set a USD invoice, the buyer can borrow USD, and an intermediary can convert the payment into RMB at settlement. The settlement currency therefore does not by itself identify the invoice currency or the borrower’s exposure.

Recipients then choose whether to hold, invest or convert their proceeds. Access to assets, liquidity, hedging, credit quality and the ability to move funds shape that choice. Liu discusses RMB green bonds as a possible asset supporting reinvestment. This is a proposed channel; a green label alone does not make a bond safe or liquid.
