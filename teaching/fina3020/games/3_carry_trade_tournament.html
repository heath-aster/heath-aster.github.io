The payoff formula from lecture
E1E0 = L [ (1 + iH) S1S0 − (1 + iF) ] + (1 + iF) | ROI = E1E0 − 1
E0, E1 = equity at the start and end of the year · L = assets ÷ E0, gross leverage, so debt = (L − 1)E0 · iH = rate on the high-yield currency you invest in · iF = rate on the low-yield currency you borrow · S = units of the funding currency per unit of the invested currency (JPY per USD here). S1/S0 below 1 means the funding currency has appreciated, which is the loss case.
In this game. The lecture formula measures the return in the funding currency. This desk reports everything in USD, so the same accounting reads:
E1 ÷ E0 = L × [ sum over USD, BRL, MXN and cash of wk(1 + ik)(X1/X0)k ] − (L − 1)(1 + iJPY)(S0/S1)
where wk is the weight of currency k, X is the USD value of one unit of that currency (X1/X0 = 1 for the USD and cash legs), and S0/S1 is how much more USD it takes to repay one JPY of debt. Cash is a leg with ik = 0.

How the wipeout distance is worked out
Routine desk stress test: EM currencies −8%, JPY +6%
Your ROI in that case: −7.05%. This is the sort of move a desk checks every morning. It is not a forecast, and it is not the event in Stage 3.
If those numbers are larger than you want to live with, you have three levers:
  • Leverage. Cutting L shrinks both the debt you owe in JPY and the assets exposed to FX, so the loss per point falls roughly in proportion. It also cuts the quiet-market ROI in the same proportion.
  • Mix of targets. Moving weight from BRL and MXN to USD removes asset-side FX risk, because USD assets have no currency mismatch with your USD reporting. You give up most of the spread: BRL pays 14.00%, USD pays 3.75%.
  • Cash. Leaving assets in USD cash at 0% removes asset-side FX risk without any credit or rate exposure, but does nothing about the JPY debt, which is the other half of your risk.

No margin rule is modelled here, so a portfolio with a thin wipeout distance may in practice be closed out by a margin call well before equity reaches zero.

Step-by-Step Carry Trade Math Guide

1. Total Assets Funded (V):

Portfolio Size (V) = Equity (E) × Leverage (L) = $100,000 × L

2. Borrowed JPY Debt (D):

Debt USD = V − E = $100,000 × (L − 1)
Debt JPY = Debt USD × 150.00 JPY per USD

3. Weighted Target Yield (itarget):

itarget = (wUSD × 3.75%) + (wBRL × 14.00%) + (wMXN × 6.50%)

4. Return on Equity with Unchanged FX (ROE):

ROE = (Annual Interest Income − Annual JPY Debt Cost) ÷ Equity
ROE Formula = L × itarget − (L − 1) × 1.00%

Which response wins depends entirely on the scenario. De-leveraging is the best of the three in this stress case and the worst of the three if the sell-off reverses, because it is the response that gives up the most carry. There is no response that is right before you know the state of the world, which is the reason a desk sizes its risk in advance rather than reacting to it.

Did the risk panel warn you?

The routine stress test in Stage 2 used a smaller move than the one that actually arrived. That gap is the peso problem from lecture: the scenario that does the damage is usually larger than the one in your historical sample, so a stress test calibrated to a quiet period understates the tail. Note also that the loss is not symmetric. Carry returns are negatively skewed, with many small gains interrupted by a few large losses.

Two-scenario calculation: expected ROI = (1 − p) × quiet ROI + p × stress ROI. The zero-ROI probability is quiet ROI ÷ (quiet ROI − stress ROI), when it lies between 0% and 100%. This is total USD ROI, not excess return over USD cash. The quiet scenario keeps the original portfolio; the stress scenario uses your response.

Which risks did the trade retain?

BRL and MXN depreciation reduce the USD value of the assets. JPY appreciation increases the USD cost of debt repayment. Selling EM assets and repaying JPY reduces both exposures; merely switching EM assets to USD leaves the JPY debt exposure. A forward to buy JPY can fix that repayment cost, but its forward rate offsets the covered interest advantage under CIP. A JPY call can limit that risk while retaining upside, at the cost of an option premium. Neither hedge removes every risk in the EM assets.
